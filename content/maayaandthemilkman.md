Maaya and the Milkman

A gloomy evening, a short encounter and a snail

Jul 03, 2025

---

It was 7:30 in the evening. Sun sets early here in the east, so it was dark, with a few vagabond fireflies twinkling on a nearby jackfruit tree.

A snail was crawling under the tree, leaving behind its slimy trace as it moved forward in its unhurried journey.

Recent days had left me in a position of sadness and melancholy. So I sat brooding on the stairs to the entrance — partly soaked in regret and partly drenched in remorse.

Where am I headed? Why am I even here? Do I serve a purpose?

I was questioning myself, unable to arrive at an answer.

My train of thought, which was speeding oblivious, got interrupted by the ring of a cycle.

The milkman had arrived. A smile on his face hinted for me to bring a vessel for him to pour the milk into. I stood up, brought a bowl, and took the milk.

One of the benefits of living in a small locality is that you are not a hard-to-remember dramatis personae of a complex novel. Everyone knows everyone, at least by name.

The milkman too knew my name.

He struck up a conversation asking about the weather, which had been stormy for the past two days. Late April is usually when Bengal witnesses Kal Baishaki — the sudden change in weather with strong winds and thunderstorms.

Swaying from this small talk on weather, the milkman suddenly said:

«“Bhogobaner haath sobar upori aache. Ei maayar prithivi te keo kichu niye aase ni re, shudu kormer goti ta badhiye jete hobe. Ekhon chollam, kal ke dekha hobe.”»

«(Trans. — His hand blesses everyone. In this world of illusion, nobody has come with any possessions, dear. We must all maintain the pace of our karma. I'll take your leave now, see you tomorrow.)»

What he had said is probably something everyone in a typical Indian household keeps listening to once in a while.

But often, when one is vulnerable, one truly understands the value of the dictums which he hitherto disregarded as cliché.

What struck me was a phrase I hadn’t heard before:

«“pace of karma.”»

What did he mean by that?

He had just left when a ripe jackfruit fell from the tree.

On the very snail which was just crawling under it a few seconds ago.

The soft, glistening trail ended mid-motion, like a sentence abruptly torn from the page.

Its unhurried journey had come to a still, quiet end.

Final. Absolute.

I was still registering the milkman’s words, and it seemed as if, in continuation, nature itself chose to complete his sentence:

«That’s all the time you would get.»

This simple encounter didn’t really resolve my questions.

Nor did it put me into any colossal philosophical pondering about something experts in this field call “Nature of Reality.”

But something quiet had shifted.

The rippling water underneath my boat had now turned into a gentle stream which nudged my boat forward — still uncertain, but moving.

And perhaps, that is how journeys begin —

Not with clarity, but with the simple, fragile courage to drift forward.
